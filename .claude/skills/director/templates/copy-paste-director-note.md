DIRECTOR BRIEF: match the style of these reference videos

You are the director, cinematographer and editor. First study the reference videos below and pin down exactly how they look and move. Then direct new videos that could sit in the same series. What matters most: their transitions, their camera angles and movement, and their lighting.

====================================================================
STEP 1: STYLE DNA (watch all three, write this before directing anything)
====================================================================

A. TRANSITIONS: list every notable transition with its timestamp. For each, write:
   - Type: hard cut on action, match cut (shape / position / motion / colour), whip pan, object or body wipe (something crosses the lens), push-through or zoom-through (camera dives into a dark or bright area), light flash or burn to white, flare transition, morph, speed ramp into the cut, dissolve, smash cut, flash frames, cut on the beat, J-cut or L-cut (sound leads or trails the picture), hidden cut.
   - The handoff: what carries across the cut (direction of motion, a shape in the same screen position, colour, brightness, a sound).
   - Why it feels seamless or deliberate.
   - How often it's used, and where (openings, drops, scene changes).

B. CAMERA ANGLES AND MOVEMENT
   - Shot-size mix as rough percentages: extreme wide / wide / medium / close-up / extreme close-up / insert.
   - Camera height and angle habits: low, eye level, high, top-down, Dutch tilt, ground level.
   - Lens feel: wide (exaggerated depth, distorted edges), normal, or long (compressed background). Shallow or deep focus? Bokeh shape?
   - Movement: locked-off, pan, tilt, push-in, pull-out, tracking, orbit, crane, handheld, gimbal float, drone, FPV, snap zoom. Give speed and direction, and what motivates each move (following action, revealing, or drifting on its own).
   - Signature framings: centred symmetry, dirty foreground, frames within frames, negative space, extreme close-up details.

C. LIGHTING
   - Key light: which side, how high, hard or soft (sharp or soft shadow edges). Read the catchlights in the eyes and the shadow under the nose.
   - Contrast: flat (~2:1), dramatic (~4:1) or noir (8:1+).
   - Colour of light: warm or cool, mixed colour temperatures, neon, practical lamps in frame.
   - Rim or back light, haze or smoke, visible beams, silhouettes, lens flares.
   - Time of day and how lighting changes between scenes.

D. COLOUR AND TEXTURE
   - The grade: warm / cool / teal-orange / desaturated / punchy / pastel / monochrome.
   - Blacks: crushed or lifted (matte). Highlights: clipped or rolled off.
   - The palette as 4–6 hex colours, and where each colour lives (wardrobe, set, light).
   - Texture: film grain, halation (glow around bright edges), bloom, motion blur, frame-rate feel.
   - Aspect ratio and letterboxing.

E. RHYTHM
   - Average shot length in seconds, and the shortest and longest holds.
   - Where cutting speeds up or slows down. Do cuts land on the beat?
   - The hook: what happens in the first 1–3 seconds.

F. SIGNATURE MOVES: the 3–6 specific, repeatable choices that make these videos recognisably theirs, each with a timestamp. "Cinematic lighting" doesn't count. Something like "every scene change is a whip pan left with a whoosh, landing on a low-angle push-in" does.

G. SHARED vs DIFFERENT: what all three have in common (that's the real style) and where they disagree. For each disagreement, pick a side and say why.

STEP 2: DIRECT A NEW VIDEO IN THAT STYLE

Brief: [WHAT THE VIDEO IS ABOUT]
Length: [__ seconds] · Aspect: [16:9 / 9:16] · Video model: [Veo / Kling / Runway / Sora / Seedance / other]

Deliver, in this order:
1. TREATMENT: logline, 3–5 emotional beats, and one paragraph on the visual approach that cites the Style DNA.
2. BIBLES
   - Character: one fixed description per character (age, build, face, hair, wardrobe with colours and materials, one distinctive detail). Paste it word for word into every prompt that character appears in.
   - Location: layout, time of day, light sources and their direction, palette.
   - Style suffix: one compact line of lens + light + grade + texture + aspect, appended to every prompt.
   - Avoid list: the things that would break this look.
3. SHOT LIST TABLE with these columns: # | seconds | beat | shot size | angle/height | lens mm | camera move (speed + direction) | ONE subject action | lighting | transition OUT (how this shot hands off to the next) | sound cue. Shot lengths should follow the rhythm from Step 1E and add up to the target length.
4. PER-SHOT PROMPTS
   - Start-frame image prompt: composition, subject (bible text), setting, light direction and quality, lens, depth of field, grade, texture, plus the style suffix.
   - Motion prompt: describe only what changes (the one action, the camera move with speed, direction and end framing). Don't re-describe the image.
   - End-frame prompt: only when a transition needs the shot to land on an exact frame.
   - Avoid list, plus continuity notes carried over from the previous shot.
5. TRANSITION PLAN: for every cut, write the last moment of shot A and the first moment of shot B as a pair (see the rules below).
6. EDIT PLAN: cut points (on the beat if the references do that), speed ramps, music tempo, sound effects (whooshes, hits, risers), grade and grain pass, titles.

====================================================================
DIRECTING RULES
====================================================================

TRANSITIONS (the hard part with AI video: plan them, don't hope for them)
- Design every transition at the shot-list stage. A transition is two shots: the end of A and the start of B, planned together.
- Match cut: end A on a clear shape, position or motion. Start B with the same shape in the same screen position, moving the same way. Use A's last frame as the composition reference for B's start frame.
- Whip pan: end A with a fast blurred pan in a direction. Start B mid-blur in the same direction. Cut inside the blur.
- Object or body wipe: at the end of A, something crosses close to the lens and fills the frame. B starts filled with the same dark or colour, and the object clears.
- Push-through: push into a dark or bright area (doorway, pupil, flare, black fabric) until it fills the frame. B emerges from that same tone.
- Flash or burn: A blooms to white (sun flare, camera flash, practical light). B starts from white.
- Speed ramp: slow down into the key moment, snap back to speed on the cut.
- Use first-frame / last-frame control when the model supports it, so A lands exactly on the handoff frame.
- Keep motion direction and brightness continuous across a cut, even when the scenes change completely.
- Only use transition types the references actually use, at roughly the same frequency.

CAMERA
- Spell it out: shot size, angle, height, focal length in mm, move, speed, direction, end framing. Write "slow push-in, about 10% closer over 4 seconds, ending on a medium close-up", not "dynamic camera".
- One camera move per shot, two at most if they're compatible. Never contradictory moves.
- Keep moves physically plausible (dolly, crane, gimbal, drone) unless the references use impossible camera moves.
- Keep the 180-degree line, screen direction and eyelines consistent within a scene.

LIGHTING
- Describe light by direction, quality, contrast ratio, colour and motivation. For example: "hard warm key high camera-left through a window, 4:1, cool bounce fill, light haze, rim light separating her hair from the dark background."
- Put the lighting in the start-frame image prompt. The video model inherits it from the first frame.
- Keep light direction and time of day continuous within a scene. Change them only on purpose, at a scene change.

AI VIDEO GENERATION
- Start frame first: generate a high-quality still, then animate it. Iterate on stills; they're cheap.
- One action per shot. Split complicated beats into several shots and cut between them.
- No readable text inside generations; add titles in the edit. Keep hands simple or out of focus. For fast action, generate slow motion and speed it up in the edit.
- Generate 2–4 takes per shot and pick on physics and performance, not just looks.
- Finish with one colour grade and one grain pass over the whole edit so every clip feels like the same film.

BEFORE YOU HAND IT OVER, CHECK AND FIX
- Every transition is planned on both sides of the cut.
- Every shot has exactly one action and one clear camera instruction.
- Light direction and time of day are continuous within each scene.
- Character and location descriptions are identical in every prompt.
- Shot durations add up to the target length.
- Every signature move from Step 1F appears at least once, on purpose.
